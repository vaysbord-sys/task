# Canon — The Bar, and How It's Actually Built

## 1. Anatomy of a campaign

Every piece of work that lasts has the same skeleton. When a piece feels thin, one of these links is missing — find which one before adding design.

| Layer | The question | Failure smell |
|---|---|---|
| **Business reality** | What must change commercially? Attendance, subscriptions, new audience, relevance in a market? | "Awareness" |
| **Audience** | Who specifically, and what do they believe *right now*? | "Everyone", "Gen Z" |
| **Human truth** | What's true about them whether or not this brand exists? | A stat wearing a truth costume |
| **Tension** | The friction between what they want and what's in the way. This is the engine. | No conflict → no drama → no work |
| **Brand right** | Why is this brand the only one allowed to say it? | Could be a competitor with a logo swap |
| **Platform** | The organizing idea. A place to make work *from*, for years. | It's actually just a headline |
| **Line** | The compressed expression of the platform. | Describes rather than takes a position |
| **System** | Type, color, motion, layout, naming — the rules that let others execute it. | Beautiful one-off, unrepeatable |
| **Surfaces** | Where it lives: film, OOH, stadium, jersey patch, app, social vertical, ticket, retail, sonic. | All film, no system |
| **Proof** | What makes it credible: a mechanic, an act, a product change, a partnership. | Slogan with no behavior behind it |

**The stretch test.** A platform is real if you can generate ten new executions from it in ten minutes without repeating a beat. If you can only get three, you have a campaign; if you can only get one, you have an ad.

**The behavior test.** The strongest platforms make the brand *do* something, not just say something. An act — a rule change, a program, a product, a giveaway, an access mechanic — is worth ten films.

## 2. Sports leagues

**The structural fact:** a league's in-house creative team is a design *system* operation wearing a campaign hat. It serves a fixed calendar, dozens of internal stakeholders, 30+ club brands with their own equity and their own fans, broadcast and sponsor obligations, and a global expansion agenda — with a small team, every week, in season. Work that ignores this reads as an outsider immediately.

**How league creative is actually organized:**
- **Tentpoles on a calendar.** Season kickoff, draft, holiday windows, playoffs, championship, international windows, all-star/pro-bowl moments. Each needs an identity that is *of* the league and *of* the host city or moment. The draft identity is the clearest annual example: a new visual system every year, rooted in the host city's culture, that still reads as the league's.
- **Club-flex systems.** The league sets the frame; clubs fill it. The hardest, highest-value craft problem in league design is a system that survives 32 color palettes and 32 fanbases without turning to mush. Show this and you're speaking their language.
- **Ritual over novelty.** Leagues own habits: Sunday, the anthem, the tailgate, the group chat, the rivalry week. Ritual is the deepest equity a league has and it renews annually for free. Campaigns that respect ritual travel; campaigns that ironize it get punished by fans.
- **The player is the medium.** Personality, celebration, style, tunnel-walk fashion, the crossover into music and streetwear. The modern league's growth engine is athlete culture, and clearance/likeness reality shapes every execution.
- **Growth agenda, always.** International markets and games, women's fandom, youth participation pathways (flag football's Olympic inclusion for LA28 is the current structural bet), and streaming-native distribution. Any idea that also serves a growth priority gets funded faster than one that only serves brand.
- **Localization as craft.** The same tentpole must work in a second language, a different sport-literacy level, and a market where the brand is the challenger, not the default.

**Transferable rules:**
- Design the *system*, then show one gorgeous instance of it — never the reverse.
- Anchor to the calendar. Undated ideas are unbudgetable.
- Give clubs/partners something to do; work that only the league can execute dies in the last mile.
- Never touch the primary mark as your opening move.

## 3. Sportswear CCO thinking

The chief creative offices of Nike, Adidas, Under Armour, and Puma are the most reliable school for campaign construction in the world, because the category has no functional advantage to hide behind — everyone sells shoes, so everyone competes on belief.

**What that school teaches:**
- **Sell the self, not the product.** The purchase is aspirational identity. The product appears as evidence, late.
- **Take a position with a cost.** The work that mattered was always the work that some people hated. A campaign nobody could object to is a campaign nobody repeats.
- **Athlete as proof, not as decoration.** The athlete embodies the tension; if the idea works with any athlete swapped in, the athlete is a rental.
- **The underdog frame is the most reliable engine in sport** — but it must be earned with specificity, not "believe in yourself" wallpaper.
- **One line, worked for months.** In this category the line is a product. It gets tested by saying it out loud in a locker room and hearing whether it sounds like something a person would actually say.
- **Design language as recognition at 20 mph.** Type, silhouette, and color are built to be identified from a passing bus. Sportswear brand systems are deliberately loud and deliberately consistent.
- **Culture over category.** These brands win by living in music, style, and internet culture rather than in "sports advertising."

**Transferable rules:**
- Write the line before the layout. If the line is limp, no art direction saves it.
- Find the enemy — complacency, the establishment, the "right way" to do the sport.
- Make the work sound like the audience talks, not like a brand talks.

## 4. Tech design leadership

The Apple/Google design-director school contributes the *opposite* discipline to the sportswear school, and the best portfolio pieces have both: heat and control.

**Apple lineage — reduction and inevitability:**
- Remove until the remaining thing looks like the only possible answer.
- One typeface, an obsessive type scale, enormous restraint with color, and photography that treats the object as the hero with nothing competing.
- Copy is short, declarative, and confident. No exclamation points, no adjectives doing structural work.
- Motion exists to explain a spatial relationship, never to entertain.
- The product page *is* the argument — sequence, pacing, and reveal are the design.

**Google lineage — systems and clarity at scale:**
- A design language is a contract that lets thousands of people build consistent things: tokens, spacing scale, elevation, states, semantic color.
- Accessibility as a first-class craft input, not a compliance pass — contrast, focus, motion sensitivity, legibility at small sizes.
- Playfulness inside strict structure: the system holds, the expression varies.
- Show the rules, not just the artifact. A page that documents its own logic reads as leadership.

**Transferable rules:**
- Hierarchy is a decision about what the viewer must miss. Make it.
- One easing curve, one grid, one type family, three sizes — then break exactly one rule, on purpose, once.
- If a section needs a caption to explain what it is, the design failed.

## 5. Agency canon

Two different traditions, both needed.

**The idea shops** — Wieden+Kennedy, Droga5, BBH, Mother, 72andSunny (the NFL's own brand agency for its Super Bowl work), Uncommon.
- The idea is a *thing that happens in the world*, not a message that gets delivered. Acts, stunts, products, rule changes, donations, formats.
- Cultural timing: the work is designed to enter a conversation already in progress.
- Craft in film: casting, music, edit rhythm, and a first frame that stops a thumb.
- The brief is written until it's dangerous. "Bland brief, brilliant work" is a myth.

**The identity shops** — Koto, Red Antler, Mythology, Collins, Pentagram, Gretel, Order, Athletics.
- **Koto**: systems built to be used — a warm, modern, extremely coherent toolkit with clear rules, motion, and an obvious logic for how it flexes across products and surfaces.
- **Red Antler**: brand as a *reason to exist* — positioning, name, and the launch narrative fused; the identity is designed backwards from the go-to-market moment.
- **Mythology**: narrative-first — the brand's story and belief system precede the mark; strategy you can read as prose, not as a matrix.
- **Collins**: transformation with heat — bold, expressive, culture-facing identity that behaves more like a campaign platform than a style guide.
- **Pentagram**: the mark and the reasoning behind it, presented with total clarity; the idea is defensible in one sentence and one drawing.

**Transferable rules:**
- Case studies from these shops are structured as *arguments*: context → problem → insight → idea → system → proof. Copy that structure for the page.
- Show the system's rules alongside the beauty shots. Rules are what a director is hired to set.
- Name things well. A named mechanic ("the ___ program") is more memorable and more forwardable than an unnamed good idea.

## 6. The line

A line is not a summary. It's a compression of a position that generates behavior.

**Diagnostics — kill the line if:**
- It could sit under a competitor's logo unchanged.
- It describes what the thing *is* rather than what it *demands*.
- It contains "your," "the power of," "where ___ lives," "more than a game," "redefining," or "elevate."
- No one would say it out loud to a friend.
- It doesn't survive translation to a second language and a second market.
- It generates no second execution — one poster and done.

**Tests to pass:**
- **Verb test.** Can it be acted on? Lines with an implied action outlive lines with an implied mood.
- **Argument test.** Does it stake something a reasonable person could disagree with?
- **Chant test.** In sport especially: could 60,000 people say it? Rhythm, stress pattern, and stops matter more than cleverness.
- **Room test.** Say it flat, with no music, to a skeptical person. If it needs the film to work, it isn't the line.

## 7. Failure modes

The specific ways spec work for a major brand dies on arrival:

1. **The rebrand ambush.** Redesigning their logo or a club's identity uninvited. Reads as ignorance of what the equity is worth.
2. **The vibe deck.** Beautiful imagery, no strategy. First question in the room — "what's the idea?" — has no answer.
3. **The audit trap.** Twelve slides of what's wrong with their current work before showing anything of your own. Nobody hires the person who opened with a critique of the team he wants to join.
4. **Trend cosplay.** The current visual meme (chrome, brutalist, Y2K, AI-slop gradients) deployed with no reason it's right for *this* brand.
5. **Unmakeable work.** Ideas that ignore rights, clearance, sponsors, or the fact that a small team must ship it weekly.
6. **Portfolio-first pages.** Bio, skills, résumé, "I'm passionate about storytelling." The work should be the argument; the person is a footer.
7. **Copy nobody would say.** Headline written in brand-voice-generic instead of in the voice of a real human being with a position.
8. **One surface.** A single hero film mocked up five ways is not a platform; it's a poster with variants.
9. **Fake metrics.** Invented results ("+340% engagement") in a spec case study. Insiders spot it instantly and it torches credibility. State clearly that the work is conceptual and unaffiliated.
10. **The link that doesn't load.** Slow, broken on mobile, or gated. The craft argument dies before the first sentence.
