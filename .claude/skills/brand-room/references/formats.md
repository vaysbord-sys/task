# Formats — What Gets Built

Architectures for the artifacts this room ships. Use them as skeletons, not scripts: every section listed has a *job*, and if a section can't do its job for this particular idea, cut it. A short piece with nine excellent screens beats a complete one with fifteen adequate ones.

## 1. The spec-work landing page

The default deliverable. One scrolling page, ~7–10 sections, readable in 90 seconds and re-readable in five minutes.

| # | Section | Job | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hero** | Say the idea | Full viewport. The line, big. One supporting sentence. Nothing else — no nav, no logo wall, no scroll cue that's doing the work the layout should do. |
| 2 | **The tension** | Earn the idea in 40 words | One paragraph or three short lines. State the friction, not the opportunity. This is where a strategist's fingerprints show. |
| 3 | **The platform** | Name it and define it | The line, what it means, and what it gives the brand permission to do. Add the brand-right sentence: why only they can say it. |
| 4 | **The work** | Prove it exists | 4–8 executions, art-directed, each with a one-line caption naming the surface (film, stadium, app, jersey, ticket, vertical cut). Show range of *kind*, not five posters. |
| 5 | **The system** | Prove it scales | The rules: type, color, motion, layout, naming. Show it flexed — by club, by market, by language, by format. **This is the section that separates a CD from an AD; never cut it.** |
| 6 | **The act** | Prove it behaves | The thing the brand *does*, not says: a program, a mechanic, a product, a partnership, an access play. One screen, concrete. |
| 7 | **The calendar** | Prove it's runnable | Where it lands against their real season/tentpoles. Dates make an idea budgetable. |
| 8 | **How I'd run it** | Prove leadership | Short: team shape, in-house vs. agency split, what ships week one, what compounds by year three. Four lines, no fluff. |
| 9 | **Close** | Make replying easy | The line once more, the conceptual/unaffiliated disclaimer, name, one contact. Small. Confident. |

**Rules of the format**
- Sections 5–8 are what almost nobody sends. They're the reason this gets forwarded.
- No nav bar unless the page is genuinely long; a scrolling argument shouldn't offer exits.
- No "About me" section. Ever. The footer line is the whole biography.
- If the piece involves a second audience (a club, a partner, a market), give it one screen, not a whole parallel track.

## 2. The short presentation

When the target expects a deck, or when the idea is better paced as discrete beats. Build it as **a browser-native deck** — one HTML file, arrow-key/swipe navigation, one idea per screen — not a PowerPoint, unless a `.pptx` is explicitly requested (in which case use the `pptx` skill and keep this structure).

1. **Title** — the line, and nothing that looks like a title slide.
2. **The problem** — the business reality, in one sentence and one number if a real one exists.
3. **The tension** — the human friction. The turn of the deck.
4. **The idea** — the platform, named. Big type, no bullets.
5. **What it makes true** — three consequences of believing it.
6–9. **The work** — one execution per screen, one caption each.
10. **The system** — the flex: club / market / format.
11. **Year one** — calendar and the act.
12. **Close** — the line, the disclaimer, the contact.

**Deck rules:** one thought per screen; no slide has both a headline and a paragraph and a chart; type is the design; a screen that needs to be read for more than eight seconds is two screens.

## 3. The case-study page

For *existing* work being presented at this bar. Same craft, different spine — and the honesty rules tighten because real results are being claimed.

**Context → Problem → Insight → Idea → System → Execution → Result → Role.**

- **Role** is mandatory and specific: what the user personally did versus what the team did. Vagueness here is the fastest way to lose a senior reference check.
- Results: only real, sourced numbers. If numbers aren't shareable, say so plainly — "results under NDA" is more credible than an invented percentage.
- Process artifacts (sketches, kills, the version that didn't work) are worth more to a hiring creative director than another beauty shot. Show one kill and why.

## 4. The pitch note

The 60–90 words that carry the link. This is a deliverable, not an afterthought — most spec work dies in the email, not on the page.

**Structure:** what you made (one line) → why you made it / the idea in a phrase (one line) → what you'd do next (one line) → link. No résumé language, no "I'd love the opportunity," no attachments.

**Example shape:**

> I didn't send a portfolio. I made something for [tentpole/problem].
> [Line] — the argument being that [tension in eight words].
> It includes the system and how I'd run it across [their real constraint].
> [link]
> Conceptual, unaffiliated, made because the problem was interesting.
> — [Name]

Adapt tone to the channel (LinkedIn DM shorter, application field slightly more formal), but never inflate it. Confidence is compression.

## 5. Naming and delivery

- **Name the campaign, then name the file after it.** `no-neutral-ground.html` reads as work; `nfl-portfolio-v3-final.html` reads as an application.
- Give the page a `<title>` that is the campaign name, not "Portfolio." It's what shows in the browser tab when it's forwarded — and it's what shows in the Artifact gallery.
- One link per target. Multiple links split attention and halve the odds either gets opened.
- Keep every target's piece in its own directory in the repo so past work is reusable and versioned.
- Ship the pitch note alongside the link in the same handoff, so the user can send it in one move.
